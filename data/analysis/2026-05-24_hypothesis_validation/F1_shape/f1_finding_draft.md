# Finding 1 · The Shape of Default Blindness

## §4.1 Result

In the baseline condition (no app, 1,000 agents, 14 days, two seeds pooled), the population-level
**notice rate is 9.5%** — of every 100 moments in which two residents are physically co-present
(within 5 m, same 5-minute tick), only 9 to 10 of them clear the attention gate to register as
mutual perception. Ninety percent of the physical neighbourhood is not socially visible.

This headline figure conceals two structurally important distributions.

**Individual axis (Figure 1A).** Across 1,772 agent-seed observations (each agent retained if it
accumulated ≥ 10 encounters), individual notice rates approximate a right-skewed distribution
centred at a median of 9.4 % (mean 9.9 %, sd 5.1 pp). Three quarters of the population sits
between 6.9 % and 12.0 %; the tails are sparse but meaningful. A **deep-blindness floor** of
2.5 % of agents register a notice rate below 2 % — these are residents who, across two simulated
weeks, look up at fewer than 1 in 50 of the people they physically pass. At the opposite end, a
**high-awareness ceiling** of 1.0 % of agents exceed 30 %. The maximum is 60 %. The distribution
shape suggests blindness is not a uniform population trait but a steep individual gradient.

**Spatial axis (Figure 1B).** Pooled across 564 locations with ≥ 40 encounters, the notice rate
varies by a factor of roughly three across building-type categories. **Residential locations
(notice rate 19.4 %)** are the awareness islands: small repeat-encounter pools (same lift, same
lobby, same neighbour-encountered-while-getting-mail) produce the highest sustained mutual
perception. They are followed by **worship spaces (17.2 %)**, **playgrounds (14.0 %)**, and a
broad commercial / office / hospitality band at 12–14 %. At the opposite extreme, **street
segments register only 7.1 %** — the canyon of urban blindness. Streets carry 113,162 encounters
in this sample (the largest single-type pool), yet they convert the lowest share into awareness.
The top 15 individual locations are all residential buildings or small playgrounds (max 45 %);
the bottom 15 — Tantallon Road, Tambourine Bay Road, Longueville Road, Austin Street, several
small playgrounds — return precisely 0 % across 40 to 194 encounters each.

## §4.2 Discussion

The literature on urban density treats co-presence as the foundation for social interaction
(Jacobs 1961; Granovetter 1973; Sevtsuk 2019). Bluetooth-proximity and check-in studies have long
documented that physical co-presence is a *necessary but insufficient* precursor to interaction
(see Sevtsuk et al.'s 2019 PLOS One framing). Direct quantification of the gap, however, has been
largely unavailable: real-world instruments observe presence or, separately, observe interaction,
but rarely capture the moment-by-moment attention threshold that links the two.

Finding 1 places the gap empirically. Three properties matter.

First, **scale**: a 90.5 % default loss rate is roughly an order of magnitude larger than what a
Jacobsian sidewalk-ballet imagination would assume. The Hudson Street choreography that Jacobs
described — a hardware store owner, a super's wife, school children passing in three directions —
implicitly trusts mutual awareness as the default. The baseline data here suggests that under
contemporary attentional conditions, default awareness is residual rather than foundational.

Second, **dispersion**: blindness is not a population property but an individual gradient. The
3 % at the deep-blindness floor see, effectively, no one across a fortnight in their own
neighbourhood. This is not a personality artefact — it correlates only weakly with extraversion
or openness (see Finding 5). It correlates more strongly with day-to-day cognitive availability
(routine adherence, schedule autonomy), suggesting that contemporary blindness is structurally
imposed rather than dispositionally chosen.

Third, **geography**: blindness has a place-shape. Streets — the very surfaces Jacobs assumed
to be the urban stage — are the lowest-conversion locations in the data (7.1 %, less than half
the residential rate). This inversion is consistent with the smartphone-era observational
literature (Brown & McGill on smartphone walking behaviour; Lu 2024 on eye-tracker pedestrian
attention): the street is now a venue of attentional withdrawal, not attentional engagement.
Residential lobbies and worship spaces — small repeat-encounter pools — preserve the older
proximity-equals-awareness regime that streets no longer support.

Taken together, the default state of the simulated Lane Cove is a neighbourhood in which the
**street fabric is mostly invisible to its users**, while residual awareness clusters in small,
familiar interior pools. The interventions tested in Findings 4 and 5 do not act on a single
"awareness lever"; as the subsequent sections show, they reshape this distribution unevenly along
both axes.
